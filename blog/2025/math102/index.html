In today’s post, we’ll explore matrices and cosine similarity. We’ll also discuss some applications in AI including background substraction, traffic sign matching, and K-nearest neighbors.

All data can be transformed into vectors, so Linear Algebra can handle most of the real-world information.

Vector and Matrix

A vector is a collection of real numbers and has a length of n, that is, \(\mathbf{u} = \vec{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^n\).

A matrix is a collection/generalization of vectors and has m rows and n columns, that is, \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} &\cdots& a_{1n} \\ \vdots &\ddots& \vdots \\ a_{m1} &\cdots& a_{mn} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\).

Mathematically, a linear equation is an equation where the variables/unknowns’ power is at most one. Its graph for one variable is called a line, for two variables is a plane, and for more than two is a hyperplane. For example, if $x_1, \cdots, x_n$ are variables, then some linear equations are

\[\begin{align*} y &= a x + b \\ y &= a_1 x_1 + a_2 x_2 + b \\ y &= a_1 x_1 \cdots a_n x_n + b. \end{align*}\]

System of Linear Equations

Assume that a company’s sales depends on their advertising expenses on TV, radio, and newspaper and specifically computed based on the linear equation formula

\[\begin{align*} S &= a_1 T + a_2 R + a_3 N &+ b \\ \text{Sales } &= a_1 \text{TV} + a_2 \text{Radio} + a_3 \text{Newspaper} &+ b \end{align*}\]

where $a_1, a_2, a_3, \text{ and } b$ are fixed for specific sets of T, R, and N. If we know all these parameters and expenses, we can easily figure out S.

For the sake of simplicity, we can exclude b or set b to be 0. If there are three sets of T, R, N, and S, we can construct a system of linear equations

\[\begin{align*} a_1 T_1 + a_2 R_1 + a_3 N_1 = S_1 \\ a_1 T_2 + a_2 R_2 + a_3 N_2 = S_2 \\ a_1 T_3 + a_2 R_3 + a_3 N_3 = S_3. \end{align*}\]

We can also rewrite this system using vectors

\[a_1 \begin{bmatrix} T_1 \\ R_1 \\ N_1 \end{bmatrix} + a_2 \begin{bmatrix} T_2 \\ R_2 \\ N_2 \end{bmatrix} + a_3 \begin{bmatrix} T_3 \\ R_3 \\ N_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} S_1 \\ S_2 \\ S_3 \end{bmatrix}\]

and matrix

\[\begin{bmatrix} T_1 & R_1 & N_1 \\ T_2 & R_2 & N_2 \\ T_3 & R_3 & N_3 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} S_1 \\ S_2 \\ S_3 \end{bmatrix}.\]

For this matrix-vector calculation, each linear equation or each entry in the resulting vector is obtained by multiplying the corresponding row of the matrix with the vector and summing the results. Each multiplication is known as dot product, which will be introduced later.

Generally, we can have more unknowns $a_i’s$ than equations or vice versa and, regardless of circumstance, write

\[A \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\]

where $A$ is a data matrix, $\mathbf{x}$ is an unknown, $\mathbf{b}$ is a data vector, and the number of columns in $A$ must match that of the unknown vector $\mathbf{x}$.

If we have more unknowns than equations, then there are infinitely many solutions. In such cases, our goal is to figure out the most optimized solution.

Vectors Operations

For scalar $\alpha$, \(\mathbf{u} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\), and \(\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} v_1 \\ \vdots \\ v_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\), we have the following vector operations.

Vector Addition
  • element-wise addition
  • $+$
  • np.add(vec1, vec2)
  • np.add(vec2, vec1)
$$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 + v_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n + v_n \end{bmatrix}$$ $$\mathbf{u} + \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} + \mathbf{u}$$
Vector Subtraction
  • element-wise subtraction
  • $-$
  • np.subtract(vec1, vec2)
$$\mathbf{u} - \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 - v_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n - v_n \end{bmatrix}$$

  • $*$

$$\mathbf{u} \odot \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 v_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n v_n \end{bmatrix}$$ $$\mathbf{u} \odot \mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v} \odot \mathbf{u}$$

$$\mathbf{u} \oslash \mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} u_1 / v_1 \\ \vdots \\ u_n / v_n \end{bmatrix}$$

Norm/Length of a Vector
  • np.linalg.norm(vec)
  • normally use squared norm $\left \lVert \mathbf{u} \right \rVert ^2$
$$\begin{align*} &\left \lVert \mathbf{u} \right \rVert = \sqrt{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{u}} \\ &= \sqrt{u_1^2 + \cdots u_n^2} \end{align*}$$


Geometrically, dot product of two vectors is based on the projection of the first vector onto the second, that is, represent how much the former points in the same direction as the latter. The more positive and higher the result is, the more similar directions they point towards and vice versa. If the result is 0, then two vectors are perpendiclar/orthogonal.

Matrix Operations

For \(A = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} &\cdots& a_{1n} \\ \vdots &\ddots& \vdots \\ a_{m1} &\cdots& a_{mn} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times n}\), \(B = \begin{bmatrix} b_{11} &\cdots& b_{1q} \\ \vdots &\ddots& \vdots \\ b_{p1} &\cdots& b_{pq} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times q}\), and \(\mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} x_1 \\ \vdots \\ x_n \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}\), we have the following operations.

Operation Formula Commutative
Matrix Addition
  • element-wise addition
For $m = p$ and $n = q$, $$A + B = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} + b_{11} &\cdots& a_{1n} + b_{1q} \\ \vdots &\ddots& \vdots \\ a_{m1} + b_{p1} &\cdots& a_{mn} + b_{pq} \end{bmatrix}$$ $$A + B = B + A$$
Vector Subtraction
  • element-wise subtraction
For $m = p$ and $n = q$, $$A - B = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} - b_{11} &\cdots& a_{1n} - b_{1q} \\ \vdots &\ddots& \vdots \\ a_{m1} - b_{p1} &\cdots& a_{mn} - b_{pq} \end{bmatrix}$$
Transpose
  • convert rows into columns
  • mat.T
$$A^\intercal = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} &\cdots& a_{m1} \\ \vdots &\ddots& \vdots \\ a_{1n} &\cdots& a_{mn} \end{bmatrix} \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times m}$$
Matrix-Vector Multiplication
  • transform a vector using a matrix
  • np.dot(mat, vec)
  • mat @ vec
  • mat.dot(vec)
$$\mathbf{c} = A \mathbf{x} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{11} x_1 + \cdots + a_{1n} x_n \\ \vdots \\ a_{m1} x_1 + \cdots + a_{mn} x_n \end{bmatrix}$$ $$c \in \mathbb{R}^m \text{ where } c_i = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} x_j$$
Matrix-Matrix Multiplication
  • transformations at once
  • np.dot(mat1, mat2)
  • mat1 @ mat2
  • mat1.dot(mat2)
For $n = p$, $$\begin{align*} &C = AB \\ &= \begin{bmatrix} \sum_{j=1}^n a_{1j} b_{j1} &\cdots& \sum_{j=1}^n a_{1j} b_{jq} \\ \vdots &\ddots& \vdots \\ \sum_{j=1}^n a_{mj} b_{j1} &\cdots& \sum_{j=1}^n a_{mj} b_{jq} \end{bmatrix} \end{align*}$$ $$C \in \mathbb{R}^{m \times q} \text{ where } c_{ik} = \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} b_{jk}$$

Applications in AI

We know from the previous post that

  • a color image is a 3D matrix of values between 0 and 255, (Height, Width, channel) where there are 3 color channels RGB
  • a grayscale image is a 2D matrix of values between 0 and 255, (Height, Width).

Rotate Left an Image (Perpendicular)
We’ve learned how to handle a 2D matrix above, so rotating left a grayscale image should be feasible just using some above operations.

About the case of a color image—a 3D matrix, we know the last dimension is always 3 since it contains color channels. But we do want to swap its height and width. We can certainly do this using the idea of array indexing. The hint is that we can use the NumPy method np.transpose().

Convert Color to Grayscale Image

Example: Image Color Conversion
Problem statement: Convert a color image into a grayscale image.
Breakdown: Broadcasting and Matrix-Vector Multiplication $A \mathbf{x}$
  • Broadcasting can stretch a vector to be suitable for an operation.
  • The multiplication can change the shape of the matrix.
  • The resulting "vector" is obtained by multiplying the elements of each row of the matrix with the corresponding elements of the vector and summing such results.
  • The original vector acts as weights/coefficients of each row of the matrix.
Idea: To perform this conversion, we want to exterminate the last dimension. If we think of the color matrix, what we want is to suppress three channels into one in a sense that the values are still in the range from 0 to 255.
  1. Solution 1: Take their mean (without np.mean())
    2. The mean of three numbers is calculated as their sum divided by three. As such the weight for each number is $\frac{1}{3}$. Then, we can use matrix-vector multiplication on the color image and $vector = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{1}{3} \\ \frac{1}{3} \end{bmatrix}$ to create a grayscale image.
  2. Solution 2: Use standard ratio
    3. The standard ratio for color-grayscale is $[R, G, B] = [0.213, 0.715, 0.072]$. Using this ratio, we can also create a vector and apply the matrix-vector multiplication on it and a color image.
    OpenCV reads a color image in order BGR, not RGB.


In fact, OpenCV provides a function that can convert a color to grayscale image. The example above demonstrates behind-the-scene computation.

color_image = cv2.cvtColor(color_image, cv2.COLOR_BGR2GRAY)

Background Subtraction
In OpenCV, there is a function called cv2.threshold(), which works quite similarly to np.clip() or np.where() in NumPy. This function is specifically used for image data, not ndarray in general. Be mindful about the proper data type that it can handle.

import cv2

_, array_output = cv2.threshold(array_input, threshold, max_value, cv2.THRESH_BINARY)

This statement returns two outputs including the threshold and the thresholded image known as mask, respectively. Note that the threshold and max_value are numbers but need not be integers. According to THRESH_BINARY, we can interpret the condition statement that

\[\begin{equation*} output\_values = \begin{cases} max\_value &\text{if } input\_value > threshold \\ 0 &\text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \end{equation*}\]
Example: Background Subtraction
Problem statement: We want to place a foreground image into a fake background provided that the placement region in the fake background is erased before we place it.
Breakdown: Consider three input images and one output image as follows.
  • Background B: the original background without the object.
  • Fake Background F: the background that we want to place the object on.
  • Input Image I: the image with the original background and object on it.
  • Output Image O: the image with the fake background and object on it.
Idea: We first need to get the object out of the original background. Then we can place it on the fake background.
  1. Any image is a matrix of values between 0 (black) and 255 (white), so we can do matrix operations on it.
  2. Matrix operations only work when matrices are proper in the situation. So we need to make sure our image matrices are of the same size.
  3. Get the object out of the original background by taking absolute difference.
    • We can separate the object and B in I by creating a clear distinction between them. Since the only difference between B and I is the object area, we can take their difference.
    • Taking the difference only might result in negative values since the background can be darker than the object itself. So we instead take the absolute difference.
    • As such the duplicated parts result in 0, practically close to 0 due to external factors like lighting.
    We need to make values close to 0 become exactly 0 so we have the resulting image which is called foreground mask.
  4. Place the object on the fake background.
    • If the foreground mask is 0, O takes F's values.
    • If the foreground mask is 255, O takes I's values.
    Once we apply these conditions, we will obtain O.
  5. We can improve this solution.
    • We replace the absolute difference values with their (weighted) average along the color channel dimension.
    • As such, image positions being 0 on all three channels (black) are replaced with F's values.
    • Image positions being 0 on fewer than three channels are replaced with I's values.
    This technique is applied to avoid values from both F and I are mixed up.

Cosine Similarity

A loss function is a part of the model training process and cosine similarity can be used for such functions.

Dot Product

In addition to length/norm, a vector is always associated with only one direction. Two vectors can have similar or different directions. For example, scale of a vector have different length but the same direction as itself. Practically, we can think words like teachers, students, and banks. The first two may be arranged in relevant categories, so their vectors may point in closer directions. On the other hand, the last two may not.

DEMONSTRATION 3-4 images

As mentioned previously, dot product of two vectors is geometrically based on the projection of one vector onto another. This contributes to the geometric definition in the table below. Figure ? illustrates

  • the smaller the angle between two vectors is
  • the closer they are
  • the longer the projection of one vector onto the other is
  • the more they points in the same direction.

If the result is negative, their directions are different or opposite. If it is positive, they are similar or the same. If it is 0, they are perpendicular. Therefore, dot product actually represents how much one vector points in the same direction as the other. This refers to the similarity of two vectors, which goes together with cosine similarity. In fact, the definition of dot product between two vectors involves the cosine of their smaller angle.

Algebraic Geometric
\(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i\) \(\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v} = \left \lVert \mathbf{u} \right \rVert \cos{(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})} \left \lVert \mathbf{v} \right \rVert\)


Cosine Similarity

Cosine similarity is used to measure the similarity between two vectors. From two definitions above, we have the formula for cosine of the smaller angle between two vectors

\[\cos(\alpha) = \cos{(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})} = \frac{\mathbf{u} \cdot \mathbf{v}}{\left \lVert \mathbf{u} \right \rVert \left \lVert \mathbf{v} \right \rVert} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^n u_i v_i}{\sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n u_i^2} \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^n v_i^2}} \in [-1, 1].\]

Three important property for $\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v})$ are

  1. The more similar two vectors are, the closer to 1 $\cos(\alpha)$ is
  2. $\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) = \cos(a \mathbf{u}, b \mathbf{v}) \text{ for } ab > 0$
  3. $\cos(\mathbf{u}, \mathbf{v}) \neq \cos(\mathbf{u} + c, \mathbf{v} + d)$.

Above we have an example of words that shows usefulness of cosine similarity. Moreover, this concept can be applied to spot similar images.

  • We know that we can use basic operations to change brightness of an image.
  • Obviously, images with different brightness are still the same.
  • If we use absolute difference on them, a computer will distinguish them as dissimilar.
  • Using cosine similarity can address this issue thanks to the second property.

Applications in AI

A unit vector is a vector of length 1. The unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{x}$ is the vector $\frac{\mathbf{x}}{\left \lVert \mathbf{x} \right \rVert}$.

The distance between two points $x, y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ is the length of the vector from $\mathbf{x}$ to $\mathbf{y}$.

\[dist(x,y) = \left \lVert \mathbf{y} - \mathbf{x} \right \rVert.\]

While the absolute difference of two vectors is a vector, the distance between their heads (two points) is the length/norm of the resulting vector.

Traffic Sign Matching

One real-world application for absolute difference, distance, and cosine similarity is checking if two traffic signs are similar or not. They are similar when

  1. Absolute difference and distance are small and close to 0
    • Issue: The flip of an image is concluded to be more similar than its brighter image. This is because the results for the former may be smaller than that for the latter.
  2. Cosine similarity is close to 1.
    • Address the Issue: The brightness of an image has no significant effect on images’ similarity.

K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)

KNN is a popular algorithm in Machine Learning. It involves considering each piece of data as a vector and computing the distance between a testing point and training points. As such, the algorithm can select the K nearest neighbors and make further analysis to provide an suitable ouput.
