## **Basic Terminology**
### **Defintions**
**Probability** is the likehood of an event happening. 
- It is commonly expressed as a percentage $\%$ and ranges from $0$ to $1$ inclusively. 
- In daily life, we use words like "certain", "likely", "maybe" or "impossible" to express it.
- $0$ is an impossible event and $1$ is a certain event.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets\aio2025\M02W02\tuesday_probability.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1 mx-auto d-block" zoomable=true max-width="450px" max-height="350px" alt="mathematical and real-life probability vocabulary" caption="" %} 

- For example, we say, "It's unlikely to rain in the desert" or "It's impossible for the sun to rise in the east." The second statement mathematically means the probability of the sun rising in the east is 0. 

An **experiment** is a planned operation through which specific results are obtained.
- raining in the desert is an experiment
- flipping a coin is an experiment.
 
An **outcome** is a result of an experiment. The **sample space** of an experiment is the set of all possible outcomes. 
- Possible outcomes are displayed within braces $\{\}$.
- We can represent it in three following ways, which are demonstrated in later examples in this post,
    - list all possible outcomes
    - create a Venn diagram
    - create a tree diagram.

An **event** is a part/subset of the sample space. It can be any combination of outcomes.

Let's take a look at some examples. Assume that the coin, die, and card deck are fair and regular.

| Experiment                   | Sample Space       | Event                                | Probability                   |  
|------------------------------|:------------------:|:------------------------------------:|:-----------------------------:|
| Flipping a coin              | {head, tail}       | {tail}                               | $\frac{1}{2} = 0.5 = 50\%$    |
| Rolling a die                | {1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6} | {Die is even} = {2, 4, 6}            | $\frac{3}{6} = 0.5 = 50\%$    |
| Picking a card out of a deck | a set of 52 cards  | {Card is a club} = a set of 13 clubs | $\frac{13}{52} = 0.25 = 25\%$ |

### **Relations of Events** 
Consider two events A and B from the same experiment.

**Implication** <br>
- Event A implies event B means if event A occurs, then event B occurs as well.
- Mathematically, $A \Rightarrow B$ means $A \subseteq B$. 

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets\aio2025\M02W02\tuesday_venndiagram_implication.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1 mx-auto d-block" zoomable=true max-width="250px" max-height="400px" alt="venn diagram of implication relation" caption="Venn diagram of implication" %} 

**Equivalent** <br>
- Event A equals event B means A implies B and B implies A.
- Mathematically, $A \Rightarrow B$ and $B \Rightarrow A$ means $A \Leftrightarrow B$.

### **Operations on Events**
Again, consider two events A and B from the same experiment. Then there are primary operations on them as follows.

| Notation                                        | Operation Name | Description                  | 
|:-----------------------------------------------:|:--------------:|:----------------------------:|
| $A \cap B$ or $AB$                              | Intersection   | Both A and B happen          |
| $A \cup B$ or $A + B$                           | Union          | Either A or B or both happen |
| $A^\complement$ or $A^\prime$ or $\overline{A}$ | Complement     | A does not happen            |

## **Probability**
There are two ways to describe probability formula: classical probability and geometric probability.

**Classical Probability** is represented by the formula

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{number of favorable outcomes}}{\text{total number of possible outcomes}} = \frac{n(A)}{n(\Omega)}.
$$

We'll use the same event A from the above experiment and have
- $\Omega = \{ 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 \} \Rightarrow n_{\Omega} = 6$ 
- $A = \{ 2, 4, 6 \} \Rightarrow n_{A} = 3$
- $P(A) = \frac{n(A)}{n(\Omega)} = \frac{3}{6} = 0.5 = 50\%$.

**Geometric Probability**

$$
P(A) = \frac{\text{measure of domain A}}{\text{measure of domain } \Omega} 
$$

where domain is the region an event can be. 

While length measures the region in 1D, area measures the region in 2D. We'll go over an example in one dimension.

Let $x$ an arbitrary real number between 0 and 3 inclusively. What is the probability of $x$ closer to 0 than 1?
- Breakdown: Only numbers less than 0.5 are closer to 0 than 1. That is the region where $x$ can be to satisfy the condition.
- $A = (0, 0.5) \Rightarrow \text{measure of domain A} = \text{length of segment } (0, 0.5) = 0.5$
- $\Omega = (0, 3) \Rightarrow \text{measure of domain } \Omega = \text{length of segment } (0, 3) = 3$
- $P(A) = \frac{\text{measure of domain A}}{\text{measure of domain } \Omega} = \frac{0.5}{3} = \frac{1}{6}$.

### **Rules of Probability**

**Addition Rule** refers to the probability of at least one event happening. 
- Generally, we have

    $$P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)$$

    $$P(A \cup B) = P(A) + P(B) - p(A \cap B)$$ 

    $$P(A + B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(AB).$$

- If $A$ and $B$ are **mutually exclusive**---two events cannot occur simulatenously, then 

    $$P(A + B) = P(A) + P(B)$$

    since $P(AB) = 0$ by definition. 

- Since $A$ and $A^\prime$ are mutually exclusive, we can apply this rule and have the following

    $$P(A^\prime) = 1 - P(A) \text{ and } P(A) = 1 - P(A^\prime).$$

**Conditional Probability** is the probability that A occurs given that B has already happened. Imagine if you want to know whether there might be a rainbow tomorrow afternoon, it helps to know if it rains before then. The conditional probability is denoted and formulated as follows. We'll discuss in detail the second equality in **Bayes' Rule**. 

$$P(A | B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{P(A)P(B | A)}{P(B)}$$

The first equality derives from probability definition. The condition "given B has already happened" says that all the possible outcomes are from event B. The condition "A occurs" says that from those outcomes, take only the ones from event A. The figure demonstrates this idea.

{% include figure.liquid loading="eager" path="assets\aio2025\M02W02\tuesday_venndiagram_conditionalprobability.png" class="img-fluid rounded z-depth-1 mx-auto d-block" zoomable=true max-width="250px" max-height="400px" alt="venn diagram of conditional probability with B light green and A intersecting B dark green" caption="Venn diagram of conditional probability with $A \cap B$ dark green" %} 

Let's solve an example using both the idea and formula. 

| Statement                       | Definition                                           | Formula                                                                 | 
|---------------------------------|:----------------------------------------------------:|:-----------------------------------------------------------------------:|
| A fair six-sided die is rolled | $\Omega = \{1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6\}$                      |                                                                         |
| A five is rolled                | $A = \{5\} \Rightarrow n(A) = 1$                     | $P(A) = \frac{1}{6}$                                                    |
| An odd number is rolled         | $B= \{1, 3, 5\} \Rightarrow n(B) = 3$                | $P(B) = \frac{3}{6}$                                                    |
| Given B then A                  | $n(AB) = 1$                                          | $P(AB) = \frac{1}{6}$                                                   |
| Probability                     | $P(A \| B) = \frac{n(AB)}{n(B)} = \frac{1}{3}$       | $P(A \| B) = \frac{P(A \cap B)}{P(B)} = \frac{\frac{1}{6}}{\frac{3}{6}} = \frac{1}{3}$ |

<br>

**Multiplication Rule** <br>
From the conditional probability formula, we can deduce a formula for two events. The formula for a general case is provided below and will be explained in [Math for AI 103](_posts\2025-07-09-math103.md).

$$P(AB) = P(A \cap B) = P(A) \cdot P(B | A) = P(B) \cdot P(A | B)$$

$$P(A_1 A_2 \cdots A_n) = P(A_1) \cdot P(A_2 | A_1) \cdot P(A_3 | A_1 A_2) \cdots P(A_n | A_1 A_2 \cdots A_n)$$

Let's first see how conditional probability formula implies the first formula here and then take an example to see how these formulae work.

$$P(A | B) = \frac{P(AB)}{P(B)} \text{ and } P(B | A) = \frac{P(AB)}{P(A)}$$

\begin{equation}
\label{conditional-multiplication}
    \Longrightarrow P(A) \cdot P(B | A) = P(AB) = P(B) \cdot P(A | B)
\end{equation}

        <ul>

            <li> <strong>without replacement</strong> means one after another and not returning to the original pool. 
                <ul>
                    <li> Event $A_1$ is to pick the first non-defective unit. </li>
                    <li> Event $A_2$ is to pick the second non-defective unit. </li>
                    <li> Event $A_3$ is to pick the last non-defective unit. </li>
                </ul>
            </li>
            <li> Both approaches give the same result because three distinct items are chosen from the same fixed population and the order of selection does not matter. </li>
        </ul>
        <strong>Idea:</strong> The problem can be approached differently depending on the terminology and we'll go with <strong>without replacement</strong> in this solution. We pick one unit from the pool of 100 and hope it is non-defective. Then we pick another from the rest and hope the same thing. We repeat this one more time. 
        <ol>
            <li> We pick one from 100 and hope it is non-defective.
                <ul>
                    <li> There are five defectives out of 100, so there are 100 - 5 = 95 non-defectives in $A_1$. </li>
                    <li> Our chance of getting a non-defective is $P(A_1) = \frac{95}{100}$. </li>
                </ul>
            </li>
            <li> Given that the first selected product was good, we pick another from the rest and hope it is non-defective.
                <ul>
                    <li> There are 100 in total and 1 was picked, so we have 100 - 1 = 99 units left. </li>
                    <li> There are 95 non-defectives in total and 1 was assumed to be picked, so we have 95 - 1 = 94 non-defective units left in $A_2$. </li>
                    <li> Our chance of getting another non-defective given one has already been picked is $P(A_2 | A_1) = \frac{94}{99}$. </li>
                </ul> 
            </li> 
            <li> Given that both selected items were fine, we pick the last unit from the rest and hope it is non-defective.
                <ul>
                    <li> There are 99 units left and 1 was picked, so we have 99 - 1 = 98 units left. </li>
                    <li> There are 94 non-defectives left and 1 was assumed to be picked, so we have 94 - 1 = 93 non-defective units in $A_3$ now. </li>
                    <li> Our chance of getting another non-defective is $P(A_3 | A_1 A_2) = \frac{93}{98}$. </li>
                </ul>
            </li>
            <li> $P(A_1 A_2 A_3) = \frac{95}{100} \cdot \frac{94}{99} \cdot \frac{93}{98} \approx 0.856$. </li>

### **Total Probability Theorem**
:question: Using conditional probability, we can find the likelihood of an event happening provided that another event is true. Now **what if we want to find the probability of an event H and only know that several events have already occurred and they together make up the sample space?**

We can divide this problem into two stages including
- Stage 1: events $A_i$ are pairwise mutually exclusive and they combined are the sample space, which is called a **complete system**:
    - $A_i A_j = \emptyset$ $\forall i \neq j$
    - $\sum_{i=1}^{n} A_i = A_1 + \cdots + A_n = \Omega$
    - $P(A_1) + \cdots + P(A_n) = 1$.
- Stage 2: event $H$ happens given that each $A_i$ has already happened (each of them may or may not contain event H).

With this process and that $A_i$ and $H_i = H \cap A_i$ happen separately, we'll be able to obtain the desired probability. The idea is to divide event $H$ into smaller events and handle each of them. The formula for this idea is as follows.

$$P(H) = P(H_1) + \cdots + P(H_n) = P(A_1) \cdot P(H | A_1) + \cdots + P(A_n) \cdot P(H | A_n)$$

$$P(H) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_i) \cdot P(H | A_i)$$

### **Bayes' Rule** 
We can certainly pose another problem by reversing the situation from the **Total Probability Theorem** as follows.

:question: **Suppose that the probability of $H$ as a whole is known. What is the probability of $A_i$ given $H$ is true?**

Take the car shop example; we know the probability of defective units, how do we figure out which company provides more defective products in terms of percentage? Now the problem becomes a conditional probability: What is the probability of a product coming from each company given that the product is defective? 

Thus we can certainly use the result \eqref{conditional-multiplication} to obtain the following formula.

$$P(c | X) = \frac{P(X | c) \cdot P(c)}{P(X)}$$

where
- **Posterior** $P(c \| X)$ is the probability of c being true given X is true
- **Prior** $P(c)$ is the probability of c
- **Likelihood** $P(X \| c)$ is the probability of X being true given c is true
- **Marginalization** $P(X)$ is the probability of X.

If c is an element of a complete system ${A_1, \cdots, A_n}$ and X is an event $H$ with $P(A_i) \neq 0$, then

$$P(A_i | H) = \frac{P(H | A_i) \cdot P(A_i)}{P(H)} = \frac{P(H | A_i) \cdot P(A_i)}{\sum_{i=1}^{n} P(A_j) \cdot P(H | A_j)}.$$
